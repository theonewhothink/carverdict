Two cars fit the amount you can pay today. One has the lower asking price; the other appears cheaper to run. Before choosing, give both candidates the same holding period and write down what each number represents.

The calculator above uses a cash-purchase scenario. It does not fetch a valuation, an insurance premium or a repair prediction. Blank inputs stay unknown. Nothing you enter is saved or sent to an AI service.

## Start with what leaves your account

Use the actual purchase amount including the upfront taxes and fees you intend to include. Then enter an assumed net resale amount at the end of your holding period. Keep the listing price and that future assumption in different rows: one is a current quote; the other is uncertain.

For the running costs, collect your insurance quote, a maintenance plan and the recurring fees you are including. Add your own repair-spending assumption. A sum set aside in a savings account is not automatically an expense; this input represents money you assume will actually be spent during each year.

The fuel line is annual miles divided by US miles per gallon, multiplied by dollars per US gallon. Match the exact vehicle and fuel-economy figure using [FuelEconomy.gov's comparison tool](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/Find.do?action=sbsSelect). Its [cost-method explanation](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/m/help.shtml) describes its own default assumptions and how they can be personalised. The calculator here uses your entries.

## Read the breakdown before the total

Try these deliberately fictional inputs: $25,000 to purchase, $10,000 resale after five years, 12,000 miles annually, 30 US MPG and $3.50 per US gallon. Add $1,000 insurance, $700 maintenance and recurring fees, and $500 repair spending per year.

The resulting scenario is $33,000 across five years, or $550 per month when divided evenly. That monthly figure is a planning average. It does not describe the timing of a purchase payment or a large repair.

The breakdown shows $15,000 purchase less resale, $7,000 fuel, $5,000 insurance, $3,500 maintenance and fees, and $2,500 repair spending. You can now see which assumption deserves another quote rather than arguing about a single impressive-looking total.

## Change the uncertain line first

Keep a second resale assumption and a second repair-spending assumption beside your first result. Recalculate each separately. This shows how much your conclusion depends on an uncertain number; it does not assign a probability to that outcome.

If you borrow to buy, include the actual cost of credit in a separate comparison. Do not add loan principal on top of a purchase price already counted here. The [FTC's dealer-buying guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer) explains why borrowers should examine the total financing cost as well as the monthly payment.

Finish by listing anything still outside your scenario, such as parking or charging equipment you have not included. An omitted cost remains an open question. Bring those questions to your [inspection follow-up](/guides/inspection-report-next-step/) and compare candidates using the same boundaries.
