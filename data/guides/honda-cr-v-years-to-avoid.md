If you only remember one thing about the used Honda CR-V, remember this: the model year on the registration matters more than the mileage on the odometer. Between 2012 and 2016 the CR-V accumulated roughly 400 to 670 owner complaints per model year with the United States safety regulator, scoring in the 80s on this site. In 2017 that figure jumped to 1,756, and in 2018 to 2,119. Nothing about the shape of the car changed enough to explain that. The engine did.

## What happened in 2017

The fifth-generation CR-V replaced Honda's 2.4-litre naturally aspirated four-cylinder with a 1.5-litre turbocharged direct-injection engine on every trim except the base LX. Direct injection sprays fuel straight into the cylinder, and on short trips in cold weather some of that fuel washes past the piston rings into the sump before the engine is warm enough to burn it off. Owners in the northern United States and Canada found the oil level on the dipstick rising instead of falling, the oil smelling of petrol, the engine misfiring and occasionally stalling, and the cabin heater blowing cold because the engine never reached operating temperature.

Honda's response was a series of software updates and, for some vehicles, a revised fuel-injector and a warranty extension on the engine to six years, rather than a recall. That distinction matters for how the problem shows up in the record. A recall is a campaign with a number and a completion rate; a warranty extension is a letter. The CR-V's oil-dilution problem therefore lives in the complaint counts, not the recall counts, which is why the 2017 to 2019 cars can have a low recall figure and a terrible complaint figure at the same time.

## The years that are safe

The fourth generation (2012 to 2016) is the CR-V most people are thinking of when they call the car bulletproof. Its 2.4-litre engine and five-speed automatic (a continuously variable transmission from 2015) generate the kind of complaint list every car has — an infotainment system that reboots, a vibration at idle on the 2015 cars, a door lock actuator — and nothing that leaves you stranded. The 2012, 2013 and 2016 model years score 88, 87 and 83 respectively. These are the cars to buy if you want a CR-V for the reasons the CR-V is famous.

The 2020 to 2022 cars are the recovering fifth generation. Honda had by then revised the software and the injectors on new production, the oil-dilution complaints had fallen to a fraction of their 2018 peak, and the scores climb back into the 60s and 70s. The 2020 model year also introduced the CR-V Hybrid, which pairs a 2.0-litre naturally aspirated engine with two electric motors and does not have the turbo engine's problem at all. If you want a fifth-generation CR-V, the hybrid is the version with the cleaner record.

## The sixth generation

The 2023 redesign kept the 1.5-litre turbo on the petrol cars and the 2.0-litre hybrid on the upper trims. Early complaints on the 2023 and 2024 cars are mostly electrical and infotainment — a phenomenon that appears on nearly every new generation of every car in this dataset, and one that usually fades by the third year — but the scores for those years are in the 40s, below the outgoing model. The record is too young to say whether that is a launch-year effect or something structural. Until it is, the 2020 to 2022 cars are the better fifth-generation buy and the 2013 to 2016 cars the better buy overall.

## What to check on a 2017 to 2019 car

If the price is right and you are set on one of the affected years, three things reduce the risk. First, ask for the service history and look for the software update campaign (Honda issued it under a product update, not a recall, so it will not appear on a VIN recall check). Second, pull the dipstick: oil that is above the full mark or smells of petrol is the problem in progress. Third, ask where the car lived. A car that spent its life in Arizona doing highway miles will have had far fewer cold short trips than one commuting in Minnesota, and the failure mode is almost entirely a cold-climate, short-trip one.

## Summary

Buy: 2012 to 2016, and 2020 to 2022, with the Hybrid preferred from 2020. Avoid, or price for the risk: 2017, 2018 and 2019. Wait and watch: 2023 onward.
