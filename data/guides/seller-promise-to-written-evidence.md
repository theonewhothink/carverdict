The car is appealing. You mention a warning light or a missing service invoice. The seller sounds relaxed: “We'll sort that out.”

You do not need to argue about their intention. You need to understand what would count as the issue being sorted, who would do it and what you would receive before paying. Turning a friendly sentence into specific questions makes the conversation calmer and more useful.

## Name the actual issue

“Serviced” can describe very different work. Ask what was done, when, at what mileage and by whom. An invoice for an oil change answers one question. It does not answer every question about the car.

Likewise, a dashboard light that has gone out is not itself a diagnosis or a repair record. Ask for the explanation and supporting documents. Let an independent inspector decide which further checks are appropriate; do not treat a website checklist as a technical diagnosis.

An example question is: “Can you send me the dated invoice showing the work you are describing, with this vehicle identified on it?” This is an editorial suggestion, not a guarantee that a particular document proves the car is sound.

## Separate three kinds of evidence

A verbal reassurance tells you what the seller says. A written promise records what is proposed. Evidence of completed work records something that has already happened. Keep those separate in your notes.

Ask what work is included, who pays, who performs it, when it should be completed and what happens if the agreed outcome is not achieved. Check that important promises are reflected in the documents you actually receive and understand. If the arrangement remains unclear, resolve it before proceeding.

For US dealer purchases, the FTC's guidance explains the Buyers Guide and the importance of written promises and an independent inspection. Private sales and other countries have different documents and rules. Use the relevant local consumer guidance for your purchase; do not transfer a US dealer document into a different transaction.

## A recall check needs its own follow-up

Check the actual VIN through the appropriate official service. A campaign affecting a model is different from proof that this vehicle is included, and both are different from proof that its remedy was completed.

Ask the manufacturer or dealer about applicability and the available remedy record for the actual vehicle. Keep that response alongside the service paperwork. Read [our guide to recalls, history and inspection](/guides/recalls-history-and-inspections/) for how those checks answer different questions.

## Give your inspector something specific to examine

Instead of saying “please check everything,” share the seller's explanation, the documents and the questions you still have. Tell the inspector about anything you noticed and any work the seller says is planned or complete. They can explain the scope and limits of the inspection they offer.

A useful outcome is sometimes an unresolved question. The car may still be worth considering, but you should know which part of the decision is supported and which part depends on a promise. Our [viewing guide](/guides/before-a-used-car-viewing/) helps you keep that distinction visible.

Enjoy the car's appeal. Give the reassurance a separate line in your notes. There is no need to make one substitute for the other.
