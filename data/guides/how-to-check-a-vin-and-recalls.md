The vehicle identification number is printed on a plate at the base of the windscreen, on the driver's door jamb, and on the registration and insurance documents. It is seventeen characters long, it identifies exactly one car, and it unlocks three things a buyer needs before handing over money: the car's open recalls, its history, and its exact specification. All three checks take less time than the test drive.

## Recalls

The federal regulator's recall lookup at nhtsa.gov/recalls returns every safety recall issued for that VIN in the past fifteen years and whether each has been performed. It is free, it needs no account, and MotorJury's VIN check page runs the same lookup. An open recall means a defect the manufacturer has admitted and will fix at no charge — but only when the car is taken to a dealer, and many are not. Around a quarter of recalled vehicles in the United States never receive the remedy.

What to do with the result: an open recall is a reason to require the seller to have it done before the sale, or to do it yourself immediately after. It is not, by itself, a reason to walk away — a recall is a solved problem — unless the recall is for something that has already failed (an engine, a transmission, an air bag) or the car has several outstanding, which suggests a history of neglect. Note what is not there: manufacturers handle many defects through warranty extensions and customer-satisfaction programmes that do not appear in the recall record. For those you need the dealer's service history, which a dealer of that brand can print from the VIN.

## History

A vehicle-history report from a commercial provider (Carfax and AutoCheck are the largest in the United States) costs a small fee and returns the car's title history, reported accidents, odometer readings at each recorded event, the number of owners, and whether it has been a rental, a fleet vehicle, a taxi or a lease. The two things to look for are a branded title — salvage, rebuilt, flood, lemon-law buyback — and an odometer that goes backward. Either is disqualifying at any price. A single reported accident with a repair is common and not disqualifying, but it is a reason to have the car inspected by someone who can see whether the repair was done properly. The federal government's own title database, the National Motor Vehicle Title Information System, is cheaper and covers title brands but not accidents.

## Specification

The VIN encodes the manufacturer, the model, the body, the engine and the model year, and the regulator's free decoder at vpic.nhtsa.dot.gov returns all of it. This matters more than it sounds. The problems described on MotorJury's model pages are usually specific to one engine or one transmission — the 1.6-litre EcoBoost, the seven-speed dual-clutch, the 2.0-litre turbo — and a seller's advertisement does not always say which one the car has. The decoder does. It also confirms the model year, which is the single most important fact on this site and is sometimes misstated in listings, because a car registered in January is often a model year newer than one registered the previous December.

## Putting the three together

The sequence that works: decode the VIN to confirm the year, engine and transmission; look up the model year on MotorJury to see what its record says and which components to worry about; run the recall check; buy the history report; and then, if all four are acceptable, have the car inspected by an independent mechanic with a written list of the components the record has flagged. The inspection costs between 100 and 200 dollars and it is where the year-level knowledge from the record becomes car-level knowledge about the one in front of you. A model year with a bad record contains good cars; a model year with a good record contains cars that have been crashed and neglected. The VIN and the inspection tell them apart.

## What a VIN cannot tell you

Whether the car was serviced on time. Whether the previous owner checked the oil. Whether the transmission has been replaced under a warranty extension rather than a recall. Those are in the dealer's service records, if the car was dealer-serviced, and in the seller's receipts, if the seller kept them. Ask for both. A seller who has them is a seller who cared about the car, and that is worth more than most of what the report says.

## Summary

Decode the VIN for the exact year and powertrain. Check the model year here. Run the free recall lookup. Buy the history report. Then pay for an inspection aimed at what the record flagged.
