An advert can make a car feel familiar before you have even seen it. You know its colour, the asking price and perhaps the seller's favourite angle. What you do not yet know is whether the car fits your life, whether the paperwork matches the description, or what an inspection will find.

Before arranging a long trip, make a short list of what would change your decision. A useful first conversation is about closing those gaps. You do not need to sound like a mechanic.

## Send a message that is easy to answer

Try this, adapting it to the car and your situation:

“I'm interested in viewing the car. Could you confirm the model year, exact version and mileage, and send photos of the service record and any recent repair invoices? Please cover names, addresses and other private details. Are there warning lights or faults I should know about? Would you allow an independent inspection before a purchase?”

This is an original message template, not a test that proves a seller is honest. A helpful reply gives you something to investigate. A vague reply leaves a question open.

If you are shopping at a US dealer, the FTC recommends getting the total out-the-door price in writing. Use that figure in your comparison, rather than assuming the advertised price includes all purchase costs.

## Separate answers from evidence

Make three columns on a sheet: what the seller says, what you have seen, and what remains to check. “Recently serviced” goes in the first column until you see a dated record. An invoice is evidence of a recorded job; it still needs to match the car and the work being discussed.

Record the question, not a verdict about the owner. “No invoice supplied for the stated repair” is more useful than “seller seems suspicious”. It gives you a specific follow-up and keeps your decision grounded.

If the car is far away, ask for the views that matter to you before travelling: the cabin, luggage opening and any disclosed damage. A close-up may clarify a question, but photographs cannot certify mechanical condition.

## Make the viewing serve your everyday life

Bring the things that decide whether the car works for you: your usual bag, the dimensions of a regular load, or a note about your parking space. Check access and comfort in person. A model's reputation will not tell you whether you can sit comfortably or reach the controls.

Leave room in the schedule for the independent inspection. The FTC recommends one even when a dealer says the car has been inspected or certified. Agree who will inspect it, where it will happen and what it costs before making the journey.

Avoid letting the effort of travelling become a reason to buy. Decide in advance which unanswered questions would make you pause.

## Leave with a next step

You do not have to finish the day with a purchase. A good outcome may be a shortlist with one less car, an inspection booking, or a written request for a missing document.
