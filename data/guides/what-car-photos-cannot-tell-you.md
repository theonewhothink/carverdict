You spot a car, recognise the badge and open the page. The picture looks right. It is tempting to treat everything underneath it as a description of that exact vehicle.

That shortcut is where a broad car collection can mislead you. One model name can span several generations, markets and body styles. A reference photograph is a starting point for curiosity. An advert is a starting point for checking a particular car. Neither is a specification sheet.

## Ask which car the story describes

Start with three labels: the nameplate, the generation and the exact variant. “BMW M3” is a nameplate. Our [M3 design story](/library/bmw/bmw-m3/) concerns the original E30 project introduced in 1986. That historical brief does not describe every later M3.

The same distinction matters in our [MX-5 story](/library/mazda/mazda-mx-5/), which discusses the original NA design, and our [NSX story](/library/honda/honda-nsx/), which discusses Honda's first-generation project. Read the scope beside the photograph before carrying a fact into a comparison.

If a page gives you only a catalogue identity, treat the missing generation as a question to resolve. Do not guess it from a familiar badge.

## Choose the next photograph on purpose

A beautiful front three-quarter view tells you something about the shape. It leaves the doorway, rear seating, luggage space and many details outside the frame.

For browsing, choose an angle that explains the design. With the [300 SL coupé](/library/mercedes-benz/mercedes-benz-300-sl/), an open door and a view of the sill help you understand the frame's consequences. For a prospective purchase, ask the seller for views of the actual vehicle that answer your own practical questions.

A photograph of a tidy interior cannot demonstrate how the controls work. A clean engine bay cannot establish the service history. Use the picture to decide what to ask or inspect next.

## Keep a reference photo separate from an advert

MotorJury's collection uses reference images with links to their file records and licences. They are not pictures of a car we have inspected or an offer to sell it. The credit identifies the image's source; it does not certify a vehicle.

If you are considering a listing elsewhere, confirm that the seller's photographs show the car being offered. Ask about anything the written description leaves unclear. Take your fit and access questions to the viewing, rather than deciding from a reference image on this site.

## Build a comparison that answers your question

Try a two-column note. On the left, write what attracted you: the doors, the open roof, the proportions or the engineering idea. On the right, write what still needs checking: the relevant version, seating, roof operation or actual service records.

That turns an enjoyable picture into a useful next step without pretending that a visual preference proves ownership quality. Save the cars that interest you in [your shortlist](/shortlist/), then compare their scoped design briefs.

Want to understand the design rather than buy? Ask the assistant what the linked story establishes. If the site has not researched that model yet, an honest gap is the answer.

The version examples come from the manufacturer archives linked in each design story. Photo records and their limits are explained in [catalogue and image credits](/catalogue-notes/). This guide is original interpretation of those records, not a visual vehicle-identification service.
