“It's been checked” sounds reassuring. Before relying on it, ask what was checked, by whom, and what the result actually says.

Three common checks serve different purposes: a recall lookup, a vehicle-history report and an inspection. A result from one does not finish the work of the other two. Keeping them separate makes a seller's evidence easier to understand.

## A recall lookup concerns a documented safety campaign

NHTSA distinguishes a US VIN or licence-plate lookup from a year, make and model search. The former can identify an unrepaired recall for a particular vehicle; the latter gives general results for that model.

NHTSA also lists limits. Its VIN lookup does not show recalls already repaired, some newly announced recalls before all VINs are identified, most recalls over fifteen years old, non-safety campaigns, or international vehicles. Read its current guidance before interpreting a result.

Keep the identifier out of our chat. Use the [official NHTSA lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and the manufacturer's process. If a question remains, ask the manufacturer or dealer what record would resolve it. “No unrepaired recalls found” is not a statement about all of a car's mechanical condition.

## A history report concerns recorded events

A history report can help you investigate a car's recorded past. The FTC advises getting one but says it does not replace an independent inspection. Treat the report as evidence to read, not a pass certificate.

Write down the event or gap that matters and turn it into a precise question. “What documents explain this recorded event?” is more useful than asking the seller to repeat that the car is fine.

Check that the report belongs to the vehicle you are considering. Where a report and the seller's account differ, keep the difference visible in your notes until you can investigate it. An explanation may be reasonable; it still needs the appropriate evidence.

## An inspection concerns the actual vehicle

An independent inspector can examine the car in front of them. Agree the inspection's scope with that person. Tell them about the unresolved questions, relevant documents and the way you intend to use the car.

The FTC recommends an independent inspection even for a used car described as certified. Its US dealer guidance suggests obtaining a written inspection report and estimates for necessary repairs. Ask the inspector to explain what they found and what remains outside their assessment.

Do not use a chatbot's interpretation to overrule an inspector's findings. A conversation based on public model records cannot examine the vehicle.

## Keep one evidence list

For each question, write four things: the car and version, the evidence you have, what it establishes, and the next check. For example, a model-level recall campaign can tell you what concern to investigate. The next step is checking the actual vehicle's applicability and recorded remedy.

This method also helps when there is no clear answer. “Waiting for the dealer's completion record” is a useful status. “Probably repaired” hides the missing evidence.
