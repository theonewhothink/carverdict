A report lands in your inbox. It has photographs, coloured boxes and a list of observations. You were hoping for a simple yes or no. Instead, you now have a better set of questions.

That can still be a useful result. Before you return to the seller, put the report beside the advert and the notes from your viewing. Your task is to see which claims now have evidence, which need work and which remain unresolved.

## Make sure you are discussing the same car

The US FTC recommends an independent mechanical inspection, including for dealer-certified cars, and a written report identifying the vehicle and estimating necessary repairs. It also distinguishes that inspection from a safety inspection. A history report does not replace the mechanical check. Those are the source-backed starting points; the organisation method below is our editorial suggestion.

Check the identity on the report against your candidate. If something does not match, ask the inspector to clarify it before using the findings in a buying decision. Give the inspector any seller claims that mattered to you, rather than assuming they already saw the advert.

## Sort the findings into three conversations

First, ask what the inspector actually checked. Next, discuss the work they recommend and the basis of any estimate. Finally, ask what could not be assessed within the agreed inspection.

Do not invent a diagnosis from a photograph or translate a colour into a guaranteed repair bill. Let the person who examined the vehicle explain their words. You can ask: “What further information would change your assessment of this particular finding?”

An unresolved check belongs in your notes even when the rest of the report looks reassuring. It is a gap to understand, not a reason to turn an otherwise useful report into a verdict it never offered.

## Keep the seller's response separate

A seller may agree to do work or change the price. Record that response beside the relevant finding. Ask what will be done and what evidence you will receive. Keep the original report so a later conversation does not erase what was initially observed.

For dealer purchases in the US, use the FTC guidance and the actual sale documents to understand written promises. Other markets and private transactions need their own local guidance. Our [guide to seller promises](/guides/seller-promise-to-written-evidence/) helps you prepare the questions without interpreting your contract.

## Decide which question comes first

Send the inspector your most important unresolved question before bargaining over several small items. If you cannot tell what an observation means for this car, a lower asking price does not answer it.

Bring that clarified report into your [buying brief](/buying-brief/). Add your own quotes and notes; MotorJury does not inspect the vehicle or calculate a dependable repair allowance from a generic model page.
