Two adverts are open. One car promises 30 MPG; the other says 40. The second number looks better. It may still be the more expensive choice for you.

The useful question is smaller: how much fuel would each car use for the miles you expect to drive? Answer that first. Then ask what the lower fuel bill costs to obtain.

## Compare the same thing

Match the model year, engine, powertrain and market before copying a figure. A US MPG figure uses a US gallon. A UK imperial MPG figure does not use the same gallon. City, highway and combined figures also describe different estimates. Comparing unlike labels can make a sensible car look unusually good or bad.

The EPA label distinguishes city, highway and combined estimates. Its dollar estimates use stated assumptions, not your personal driving record. The label is useful for comparing versions under a consistent method; it cannot promise the fuel bill you will have on your roads.

If your exact version is missing, keep the comparison unfinished. Do not quietly borrow the best number attached to the nameplate.

## Divide before you multiply

Here is an illustrative comparison, not a valuation of any car for sale. Assume 12,000 miles in a year and a fuel price of $3.50 per US gallon.

At 30 US MPG, that distance uses 400 gallons and costs $1,400. At 40 US MPG, it uses 300 gallons and costs $1,050. The difference is $350 a year under these assumptions.

The calculation is distance divided by fuel economy, multiplied by fuel price. A ten-point increase in MPG is not a ten-percent discount on fuel. Work in gallons and dollars so the consequence is clear.

If you drive less, the absolute saving shrinks. If fuel gets more expensive, it grows. A car with a different fuel requirement needs a different price input. Your route and actual consumption may also differ from the estimate.

## Now ask what the saving costs

Suppose the more economical candidate costs $2,000 more to buy. Keeping the other illustrative assumptions unchanged, fuel alone takes about 5.7 years to cover that premium: $2,000 divided by $350 per year.

That is a narrow break-even calculation. It does not include financing, insurance, servicing, repairs, tax, resale or the condition of either car. Changing any of those can change the overall choice. It also does not mean the premium is wasted: the other car may offer something you want. It means you have separated that preference from a fuel-saving claim.

Use the [RAV4 buying brief](/buying-brief/) if your candidates fall within its stated 2019–2020 US scope. Enter your own quotes and assumptions. When an ownership-cost input is missing, leave the ownership conclusion open.

## Take one useful note to the viewing

Write down the exact version, the economy figure and where it came from, your annual distance, the fuel price and the purchase-price difference. Add a question about how the seller's reported consumption was measured. A short record of assumptions is more useful than remembering that one car was “efficient.”

Choose the car after checking its condition and your wider costs. The fuel label helps with one part of that decision. It cannot do the inspection for you.
